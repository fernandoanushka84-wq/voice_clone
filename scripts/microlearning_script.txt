Today I want to talk about something that completely changed how I learn — and it can do the same for you.

Have you ever sat down with a book or a long lecture, felt motivated at the start, and then twenty minutes later your mind is somewhere else? You finish the chapter, close the book… and a week later you barely remember anything.

You’re not lazy. Your brain is just not designed for that style of learning.

Traditional education forces us into long sessions — one-hour classes, two-hour study blocks, entire days of reading. But science shows our focused attention on a single idea is only about ninety seconds to a couple of minutes. After that, efficiency drops hard. And if we don’t reinforce what we learned, the forgetting curve wipes most of it out in a few weeks.

So what’s the alternative?

It’s called microlearning.

Microlearning means breaking knowledge into tiny, high-quality pieces that match how the brain actually works. Instead of forcing yourself through two hours of material, you take focused five-to-fifteen-minute bursts. Sometimes even a single well-made sixty-second explanation can plant an entire concept more effectively than an hour of passive reading.

Think about it. You’ve probably experienced this already. You watch one short video that clearly explains something, and suddenly the whole idea clicks. You didn’t need a textbook. You didn’t need a full course. You just needed the right information delivered in the right dose.

That’s microlearning.

I’ve used this method for years to learn things completely outside my main field. It works. It keeps motivation alive because the sessions are short enough that you actually finish them. It creates repeated exposure, which is exactly what the brain needs to move information into long-term memory. And over time those small pieces compound into real skill and real understanding.

Here’s why this matters beyond just “studying better.”

Education is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your life. Better knowledge usually leads to better decisions, better opportunities, and better income. But most people stop learning the moment formal education ends because the old methods feel too heavy. Microlearning removes that barrier. It makes continuous learning realistic.

You can use it for languages, skills, business concepts, science, philosophy — anything. Open a short article, watch one focused video, review one flashcard set, explain one idea out loud. Do that consistently and the results compound in ways most people never experience.

The goal isn’t to replace deep work completely. When you need mastery, you still go deep. But for the vast majority of learning we do every day, short, deliberate bursts are far more effective than long, exhausting sessions that we abandon.

So here’s my challenge to you this week:

Pick one thing you’ve been wanting to learn.
Break it into the smallest useful pieces.
Spend just ten focused minutes on it today.
Then do it again tomorrow.

Don’t wait for the perfect long study session. Start small. Stay consistent. Let the science of how your brain works work for you instead of against you.
